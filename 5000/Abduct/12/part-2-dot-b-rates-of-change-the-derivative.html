12.2.b Changing measures: The Derivative.

* 12.2.b Changing Measure: The Derivative

Changing and comparing measurements are the ways we draw explanations. For example we might ask how to time on the one hand and distance on the other. To connect them we need speed: distance per time. We could have weight and volume, which we transform using density. If we put this to an equation we have a measure () that needs to be converted to () and the transformation will be a new function () which we call “the derivative”.

Derivatives

If \[f:\Omega \to [-\infty,\infty]\] is a measurable and \[E\subset \Omega\] is measurable then

\[\displaystyle \nu(E) := \int_E f\, d\mu\]%2520%253A%253D%2520%255Cint_E%2520f%255C%252C%2520d%255Cmu?scale=1) is a new measure.

This is what we use for example when we wait our averages or make a probability conditional and many other familiar measurements. In fact the converse is also true.

Radon-Nikodym Theorem

Given two measures \[\mu\] and \[\nu\], if each time \[\mu(E)=0\]%253D0?scale=1) then also \[\nu(E)=0\]%253D0?scale=1); then there exists a unique measurable function (f) such that for every measurable set (E)

\[\displaystyle \nu(E) := \int_E f\, d\mu\]%2520%253A%253D%2520%255Cint_E%2520f%255C%252C%2520d%255Cmu?scale=1).

  • When each time \[\mu(E)=0\]%253D0?scale=1) then also \[\nu(E)=0\]%253D0?scale=1), we say that “\[\nu\] is absolutely continuous with respect to \[\mu\] and write this as \[\nu\ll \mu\].
  • The unique function (f) is called the (Radon-Nikodym) derivative of \[\nu\] with respect to \[\mu\] and denote it

\[\displaystyle \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} = f\].

  • Collectively: \[\nu\ll \mu\] implies that \[\displaystyle \nu(E)=\int_E d\nu = \int_E \frac{d\nu}{d\mu} d\mu\]%253D%255Cint_E%2520d%255Cnu%2520%253D%2520%255Cint_E%2520%255Cfrac%257Bd%255Cnu%257D%257Bd%255Cmu%257D%2520d%255Cmu?scale=1). In high-school textbooks this is known as the “Fundamental Theorem of Calculus”.

Let us consider two measure: position (x) and time (. In a mechanical system whenever time stands still we cannot change position. So measuring time as 0 means position is also zero; so:

Absolute Continuity against the interval measure.

Suppose want to compare a measurement () of intervals of the real number line against the interval measure (m([a,b])=b-a). Under these conditions we can squeeze intervals (a<x_1<c<x_2<b) and shrink the interval (X=(x_1,x_2)) around the point (c). Since (m([c,c])=c-c=0) has measure 0, this process has a clear consequence:

\[\displaystyle m\left( \bigcap_{x<c<y} (x,y) \right)=\lim_{x\to y} (y-x)=0\]%2520%255Cright)%253D%255Clim_%257Bx%255Cto%2520y%257D%2520(y-x)%253D0?scale=1)

So the measurements (m) will have to have their values on these shrinking intervals shrink as well

\[\displaystyle \nu\left( \bigcap_{x<c<y} (x,y) \right)=0\]%2520%255Cright)%253D0?scale=1).

So as in important special case, suppose (f:[a,b]) with the following property for all (acb)

(_{xc} f(x)=f(c)). We say (f) is continuous on ([a,b]).

Then setting (([a,x]) := _{a}^x f(t) dt) we get this property. The condition of continuous on a closed interval has been likened by many to drawing the graph of (f) without lifting your pencil. That is, when you are headed towards a point, you reach it.

This gives you access to a powerful intuition for creating absolutely continuous measures for the interval measure: the are integrals of graphs drawn without lifting the pencil.”

Let’s take a close look at the derivative of the interval measure as this is the most historic and geometrically motivated of all the derivative measurements.

* Newton-Leibniz’s Interval measurement Theorem.

\[\displaystyle F(x_2)-F(x_1) = \int_{(x_1,x_2)} f\, dm\]-F(x_1)%2520%253D%2520%255Cint_%257B(x_1%252Cx_2)%257D%2520f%255C%252C%2520dm?scale=1) where \[\displaystyle F(x) := \int_{(a,x)} f\, dm\]%2520%253A%253D%2520%255Cint_%257B(a%252Cx)%257D%2520f%255C%252C%2520dm?scale=1).

Said in the more classical interval notation this means

\[\displaystyle F(x)\bigg|_{x_1}^{x_2}=F(x_2)-F(x_1) = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(t) \, dt\]%255Cbigg%257C_%257Bx_1%257D%255E%257Bx_2%257D%253DF(x_2)-F(x_1)%2520%253D%2520%255Cint_%257Bx_1%257D%255E%257Bx_2%257D%2520f(t)%2520%255C%252C%2520dt?scale=1) wherever \[\displaystyle F(x) := \int f(t)\, dt\]%2520%253A%253D%2520%255Cint%2520f(t)%255C%252C%2520dt?scale=1), equivalently \[\displaystyle \frac{dF}{dx}=f(x)\]?scale=1).

Example 1: The Interval Derivative of position is velocity.

In a high-school physics class you likely measured position (x(t)) at a time (t) starting from time 0. So the distance accumulates over the interval (E=[0,t)). The problem is that we can travel at different velocities (positive or negative speeds). The times we travel faster we go further.

So to approximate total distance over the time interval (E) will can get a good approximation by adding up small units in time to capture moments we travel a fixed velocities. That is we have an integral. An integral of what? Well by the definition of the derivative it is an integral of the derivative of position.

\[\displaystyle x(t) = \int_E \frac{d x}{dt} \, dt = \int_0^t \frac{d x}{dt}\, dt\]%2520%253D%2520%255Cint_E%2520%255Cfrac%257Bd%2520x%257D%257Bdt%257D%2520%255C%252C%2520dt%2520%253D%2520%255Cint_0%255Et%2520%255Cfrac%257Bd%2520x%257D%257Bdt%257D%255C%252C%2520dt?scale=1).

Now the velocity is the physical concept we need to compute this same individual summands. So the derivative must be equal to the velocity.. The purpose of (dt) is to divide up the measurements in small enough intervals that we can get measurement precise enough to measure within the margin of error.

Example 2: Interval Derivative of weight is density.

Now an example from a physical measurement: the weight (w) of an object. We can divide the weight up amongst the volume using the customary Lebesgue measure (m). For example we can take a core sample of mud at the bottom of a lake and calculate its weight as an integral (or a numerical approximation of the integral). The core sample a cylinder C with a known radius and height.

\[\displaystyle w(C) = \int_C \frac{dw}{dm}\, dm\]%2520%253D%2520%255Cint_C%2520%255Cfrac%257Bdw%257D%257Bdm%257D%255C%252C%2520dm?scale=1)

Now if we measure the weight at different heights of the cylinder then we get varied weights. The weight changes based on the density of the mud. A more dense mud will weigh more. So a suitable interpretation for the derivative of weight per volume is density.

What is a tangent line?

Sometimes we over-learn the lesson of tangents to a circle. After all the exposure tho the word tangent in mathematics is usually made along side sine, cosine and the unit circle so the confusion is understandable. But if we look to the word “tangent” in the conversional meaning we are describing a topic that begin as related but in retrospect may have diverged. So the key is that at some point the two topic did appear the same. In this same way a tangent to a curve is a line that for some period approximates the shape of the graph.

  • Tangents may not exist.
  • Tangent lines, if they exist, are unique.
  • Tangents may touch the graph in many places and may even approximate the graph in more than one place.

The Derivative as Change of Measure

Now the Newton-Leibniz theorem and its geometric conceptualization of the derivative as rate of change has been enormously valuable but there are roles for derivatives where “rate of change” is less compelling as an explanation. Take for instance measurements of chaotic data like earth quakes, stock markets, or network traffic. Below we picture the seismology of the Cabo de Gata earthquake 2025. We see the behavior is quite erratic with sharp changes in direction that make it impossible to place a straight line as local approximation. That is this curve has few discernible tangents.

The absence of discernible tangents here does not mean we should not attempt to use this as a measure compared to another. It simply means we should not compare it to an interval measure. In fact one the most important measures to compare with is the simple concentration measure (_c). The graph above indicates the first noticeable motion in the graph as the P-wave – the primary wave indicating the start of the tremor. The next measure S-wave is secondary /shear wave. The distance in time between these markers can be used to predict the distance from sensor to the origin of wave. Another common integral is an interval measure but shifted to the domain of frequencies through a process known as Fourier transforms. So in short the interval measure and its associated Riemann integral and slope of tangents interpretation is not directly useful for these data but other integrals and derivatives are essential.

Try to think of derivatives as formulas to change measures and not simply as slopes. While the slope perspective is important in many historical applications it is not in the end the only interpretation and many modern data centered goals are better understood as comparing measures, not tangents of graphs.

A bit of history

Historically derivatives were defined as the slope of tangents to a curve or instantaneous rates of change. These concepts are not immediately logical—how do you define a unique tangent to a graph without already having the slope, i.e. it makes for a circular definition. An instant in time is also not a precise concept. But its ability to solve questions made Newton (1660’s) a hero of science. Between the 1800-1900’s Cauchy, Weirestrass, and Riemann each contributed ideas to make the concept precise. Still issued persisted not least of which was the difficulty in stacking integrals measurements. While geometry suggested we can remeasure as many times as we like, the formulas of the Cauchy-Riemann method had gaps and oddities that did not match the graphical intuition.

To solve these problem Lebesgue’s integral (1900) threw the subject on its head. Instead of beginning with the concept of smaller and smaller approximations, Lebesgue classified what a measurement under the basic concept that: the whole is the sum of its parts. Once created integrals stand out as accumulation of measurements under any grouping into parts – be those small approximations or large chunks. The concept now being sound later authors such as Radon-Nikodym (1915) explored how to convert the calculus of the previous generation into this formalism.

Thes are admittedly the most recent to the list but they are extraordinarily more diverse making the concept of measurement match so many more applications that it quickly clarified the field. Here derivatives are ways to change measure, and have no need to be grounded in concepts of slopes of tangents. Its explosive use in modern probability, finance, machine learning, and data science make it the right match for the computationally minded amongst us. Even so, if your primary aims are engineering, mechanics, or classical physics then the narrower picture created by Newton and Leibniz will be enough. Just be mindful not to draw upon the wrong formulas as the answers can be quit different.

Mean Value Theorem for Interval measurements

A common re-telling the Newton-Leibniz theorem is to multiply both sides by (1/x=1/(x_2-x_1) because this has a description in terms of concepts like speed and rates of change and averages. In this form it is known as the “Mean Value Theorem” (mean is a synonym to “average”).

Mean Value Theorem

For a measurable function \[f:[a,b]\to [-\infty,\infty]\] let \[\displaystyle F(x) := \int_{[a,x]} f\, dm=\int_a^x f(t)dt\]%2520%253A%253D%2520%255Cint_%257B%255Ba%252Cx%255D%257D%2520f%255C%252C%2520dm%253D%255Cint_a%255Ex%2520f(t)dt?scale=1) then for (a=0<x_1=1<x_2<2)

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Global rate of change} & = \frac{\Delta F}{\Delta x} = \frac{F(x_2)-F(x_1)}{x_2-x_1} \\ & \overset{{\small \text{discovery}}}{=} \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{f(t)}{\Delta x}\,dt\\ & = \text{Accumulated rate of instant change} \end{aligned}\]-F(x_1)%257D%257Bx_2-x_1%257D%2520%255C%255C%250A%2526%2520%255Coverset%257B%257B%255Csmall%2520%255Ctext%257Bdiscovery%257D%257D%257D%257B%253D%257D%2520%255Cint_%257Bx_1%257D%255E%257Bx_2%257D%2520%255Cfrac%257Bf(t)%257D%257B%255CDelta%2520x%257D%255C%252Cdt%255C%255C%250A%2526%2520%253D%2520%255Ctext%257BAccumulated%2520rate%2520of%2520instant%2520change%257D%250A%255Cend%257Baligned%257D?scale=1)

Said another way, there is a point (x_1cx_2) such that

Interpreting the Mean Value Theorem in Applications

You enter the highway at 8:00 am and drive for 1 hour and exit 70 miles away. Your average speed is therefore 70 miles per hour. If the speed limit is 65 miles per hour then the traffic toll system has enough evidence to charge you with speeding. This despite having not seen you actually speed at any instant in time. Why? Well the average rate of change was 70 mph and by the Mean Value Theorem there must be some point (c) where your speed (x’(t)) (the derivative of your position (x(t)) at time (t)).

This is an example of a theoretical claim. The problem with enforcing this is that perhaps your state laws require evidence in the form of a specific photograph of an instance of speeding. That is, a computational logic might be required to enforce the law. On the other hand even that could be called into question with many practical logical concerns, such as the accuracy of the data collected. Always remember to return to the simpler lessons of logic before drawing conclusion as to the validity of some purely theoretical claim.
