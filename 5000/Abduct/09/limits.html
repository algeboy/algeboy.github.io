Limits

math156 Limits

Despite calculus jargon, there is no process to add infinitely nor to converge on a smooth shape from rectangles after an infinite journey. A simple taxi trip in a city can prove this to you. But that doesn’t mean we can’t use our imagination to help us! In fact we can show that many processes that are finite can nevertheless be done so accurately as to beat any accuracy someone challenges us to achieve. And if we have such a formula then we can simply set the margin of error to 0 and declare that as the best solution, the limit. We never go anywhere near infinity – nothing approaches infinity – but we do settle on robust answers that no one can improve. So in a sense we can declare victory over infinity. But the details matter and you can fool someone…including yourself!

*Objectives

  1. Recognize limits as approximation.
  2. Define the game of limits as a two stage game.
  3. Read the limit notation accurately.

Practical Limits

The art of taking limits is a bit of a craft but and you can do in many ways a collection of and largely niche. Calculators of the 1990’s could offer correct answers and today it is almost entirely a job for computers. The except is in what is known as “theory”. Theory is when you need to work with ideas and therefore do not have precise numbers but rather have only families with unknown variables etc. to reason about. Here however AI has done a great job of solving even those questions. If you want to enter the theory of limits chances are you will need several more years of training and it is increasingly likely that by that time the AI systems will be good enough to do the limits for you. That is we are in the age similar to the change from slide-rules to calculators, or human calculators to computers.

What software should you use? There are many from Mathematica, to MatLab, to Maple which all look like math. Packages in Python and Julia are open sources but look more like what programmers would want. Online and for free is the WolframAlpha project. (Historic note, Wolfram Alpha is what powers Apple’s “Siri” so you may have used it without knowing.). Here is a screen shot. Notice you will still need to know how to type in the words that read like the limit. If you do that wrong it cannot help you. So the your main job is to be able to accurately read a limit.

Well-known Limits

To gain a foot hold on the ideas it will help you to interact with some basic limits. Using Wolfram Alpha (or your own favorite choice) type in each of these limits and see what answer you get. Then we will go through why.

\[\lim_{n\to\infty} 17\pi r^2\] , \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1,000,000}{0.0000001 n}\], \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{an+b}{cn^2+dn+e}\]

By the way, other AI systems can do this well and can even read the symbols written as math but in my current testing I still find it can make small mistakes. I think it is worth for now that you take the trip to an older AI that has been thuroughly tested and is designed for math and practice for yourself the art of translating symbols into correct sounding math. If nothing else when working with experts you don’t want to sound dumb. This practice will help.

Now if you enter the first limit \[\lim_{n\to\infty} 17\pi r^2\] into a system correctly it might read as

“The limit as n approaches infinity of 17 pi r squared.”

The answer will be “17 pi r squared”, that is, \[\lim_{n\to\infty} 17\pi r^2=17\pi r^2\]. Why should this make sense? The hint is in the parameter (n). Since (n) is what is listed as “approaching infinity” (which we know is jargon and not actually approaching infinity, nothing approaches infinity) we are telling our limit that the variable to look out for is (n). All other values are therefore constants to this expression. Thus the pattern we are saying is to just repeat \[17\pi r^2\] for each n. Now think of the rules of the limit game. I ask you to tell me what \[17\pi r^2\] will be close to within e decimal places for all but finitely many n. Well n has no role here so you could say

\[17\pi r^2\pm \frac{1}{10^e}\]

That is, “17 pi r squared give or take e decimal places.” And that would be correct. But if I first use 1 decimal place you would then say:

  • Use e=1? “17 pi r squared give or take 1 decimal place.”
  • Use e=2? “17 pi r squared give or take 2 decimals places.”
  • Use e=3? “17 pi r squared give or take 3 decimal places.”
  • Use e=4? Look dude, it is “17 pi r squared give or take ANY decimal places.”

So the game is to lock in an answer that has a secure way to update the error to the next decimal place, then set the error to 0.

FACT. Limits of constants are the constant

\[\lim_{n\to\infty} C=C\]

Now own to \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{an+b}{cn^2+dn+e}\]. I promise if you understand this one you will understand the rest. The computers will tell you this number is 0. You should be able to tell at a glance. Why? Well here you cannot use precise numbers which may seem hard. But remember you don’t actually use () as number, you just leave it as a symbol. So don’t let that scare you. Instead lets play the game but jump to some huge decimals.

  • When we want something accurate to 100 decimal places \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{an+b}{cn^2+dn+e}\] is a fraction where the numbers n can have finitely many anomolies, that is, finitely many values that are not close to our predicted limit which we think is 0. When n is small then b and e might be comparably big and dominate the calculation. But since there are only finitely many small numbers we should expect that the game is played with huge numbers n. With huge numbers you can always find n so big that adding b or e will do basically nothing. Think of 1,000,000 + 0.0001. that number is basically a rounding error away from 1,000,000. So as n gets really large the lower order terms just dont matter.
  • That also applies to terms like (cn^2+dn=n(cn+d)). Since (cn+dcn) we can drop the d.
  • We are left therefore with the following limit \[\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{an}{cn^2}\]. Here we DO NOT DROP values. Here we should consider reducing the fraction. n/n^2=1/n. No for large n, 1/n is tiny. This whole thing gets closer and closer to 0.
