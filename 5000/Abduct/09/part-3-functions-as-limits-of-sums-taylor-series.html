Part 3: Functions as Limits of Sums (Taylor Series)

Part 3 Functions as Limits of Sums (Taylor Series)

Let us think back on what a decimal number represents.

\[\begin{aligned} 0.a_1 & = a_1\frac{1}{10}\\ 0.a_1a_2 & = a_1\frac{1}{10}+ a_2\frac{1}{100}\\ 0.a_1a_2a_3 & = a_1\frac{1}{10}+ a_2\frac{1}{100}+a_3\frac{1}{1000}\\ & \vdots\\ 0.a_1a_2a_3 \cdots a_k & = a_1\frac{1}{10}+ a_2\frac{1}{10^2}+a_3\frac{1}{10^3}+\cdots + a_k \frac{1}{10^{k}}\\ \end{aligned}\]

Where do we go from here if not “infinite” decimal numbers. Yet what does this mean for the right-hand side? There are no infinite sums, but perhaps we can use our limits to come up with such a notion.

\[\displaystyle 0.a_1 a_2 \cdots a_k\cdots = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_k}{10^k}=\lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{a_k}{10^k}.\]

What if the limits don’t exist? Once more Cauchy had an answer: just invent these as new numbers! There is a small condition to make sure the number works (known as a Cauchy condition). We wont cover this here.

Now if we add this to an integer we get all the numbers we know well

\[\begin{aligned} a_{-d}\cdots a_0.a_1a_2\cdots a_k\cdots & = a_{-d} 10^{d} + \cdots +a_{-1}10^1+a_0 10^0 + a_1 10^{-1} +a_2 10^{-2}+\cdots\\ & = \frac{a_{-d} }{10^{-d} } + \cdots +\frac{a_{-1} }{10^{-1} }+\frac{a_0 }{10^0} +\frac{ a_1}{ 10^{1}} +\frac{a_2}{ 10^{2}}+\cdots\\ & = \sum_{k=-d}^{\infty} \frac{a_k}{10^k} =\lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{k=-d}^n \frac{a_k}{10^k} \end{aligned}\]

The synthesis problem

It is clear that numbers like () and () are important and useful. Getting them locked down as decimal numbers lets us put them into service inside computations. So while Cauchy was right to invent new numbers, it left us with a problem of finding strategies to take the meaning of numbers and synthesizing them as decimals. For example, the rule of () is any decimal number (x=a_{-d}a_0.a_1 a_2) where (x^2=2). This problem is essential in the sciences and there are strategies including one called Newton’s Method. We wont explore this problem here but know that it is an important step someone must take before we can use decimal numbers as tools for general numbers.

Synthesizing sines and cosines, and other functions

Now lets take on the same strategy but this time let’s make new functions!

You have probably notice a scientific calculator has a button for (), sin(x), cos(x), exp(x) and perhaps some others. These are there for a very good reason. Even if you know what these functions mean, they are not easy to calculate for all but a few numbers. So let us take on the functions we do understand, add in limits and see if we create new functions, or recover old functions in new ways.

  • A monomial is formula (_n x^n). These have familiar shapes. Here are the first four.

(y=1=x^0)

(y=x^2)

(y=x^1)

(y=x^3)

  • A polynomial is a sum of many monomials, so (_{i=0}^n _i xi=_0x0+_1 x^1++_n x^n). Visually these functions look like combinations of the above shapes. For example (y=2x0+x1) shifts the line up by 2 and (y=x2+x3) puts the parabola (“valley” in the (y=x^2) into the flat spot of (y=x^3).
  • A series is a limit of polynomials. \[\displaystyle \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \alpha_i x^i= \lim_{n\to \infty} \sum^n_{i=0}\alpha_i x^i\]

Warning. While it is denoted with the same notation as a sum, it is not a sum but a limit. There are absolutely no infinite sums anywhere in mathematics. Even so, this notation is so compelling and simple that many of just cannot stop using it.

Once more, some limits may not exist (as we discuss below). Others may not exist as known functions. When the latter happens we can simply define them to be new functions.

Convergence

When a limit exists we say the data has converged. We saw several examples of convergence in our last unit. When we create data by adding up other data there are some patterns of convergence that emerge and are useful to know. For example, suppose we add up a constant over and over. That will become a larger and larger number and head off to infinity.

  • If \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} a_k=L\] is a finite number then \[\displaystyle \lim_{k\to \infty} a_k=0\].
  • Warning: Even if \[\displaystyle \lim_{k\to \infty} a_k=0\] it may still happen that \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} a_k=L\] is infinite, e.g. \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} \frac{1}{n}=\infty\]. This is known as the Harmonic Series.
  • \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} \frac{1}{k^p}=L\] is finite if (p>1).
  • \[\displaystyle \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^k = \frac{1}{1-x}\] when (-1<x<1). For example \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} \frac{1}{2^k}=\frac{1}{1-1/2}=2\]
  • If \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} a_k=L\] and \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} b_k=M\] then \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} (a_k+b_k)=L+M\]%253DL%252BM?scale=1) and \[\displaystyle \sum^{\infty}_{k=0} \lambda a_k=\lambda L\].
