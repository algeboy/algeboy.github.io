Probability as Quantitative Abductive Logic

The challenge to rank events (KH) is the opening we need to add mathematics to the art of abductive logic. Let us add some further conditions on this ranking which we denote (Prob(X)) of an event (X).

  • The ranking should be a total order, that is,

  • Transitive: if we rank (X) as less likely than (Y), i.e. (Prob(X)< Prob(Y)) and (Y) as less likely than (Z), i.e. (Prob(Y)Prob(Z)) then we can conclude that (X) is less likely than (Z), i.e. (Prob(X)Prob(Z)).

  • Total: Any two ranked events should be comparable, so (Prob(X)Prob(Y)) or (Prob(Y)Prob(X)).

  • Reflexive. Events are as likely as themselves (Prob(X)Prob(X)).

  • Anti-symmetric: If events that out rank each other are in fact equally likely: (Prob(X)Prob(Y)) and (Prob(Y)Prob(X)) leads to (Prob(X)=Prob(Y)).

  • A bottom rank. The absence of an event has a measure, typically denoted by 0, so (Prob()=0)

  • A top rank. The measure of all events, denoted () has a maximum measure typically denoted 1, so (Prob()=1).

It turns out that the decimal numbers ([0,1]={x x}) satisfy exactly these rules. So over the past 150 years scientist have been developing a specific plan to quantify some forms of abductive logic by insisting on a function (Prob(X)) as a decimal number between 0 and 1. Its starting point is probably something you already know.

Proportions as a first Probability

Imagine you fill a bag with red and blue balls. (In probability circles it is typical to use an urn instead of a bag.). You reach into the bag and pull out a ball. What color do you expect to see? It is hard to say. But suppose I now tell you there are 30 red balls and 10 blue. You probably expect to see a red ball as your first choice. What if I change the that 25 red and 15 blue? You might still favor red but less than before. How about 21 red and 19 blue? You might now start to return to the uncertainty of expecting red or blue.

What this thought experiment illustrates is that much of our abductive reasoning can be quantified using proportions.

  • There 10/40 blue vs. 30/40 red suggests red is more plausible than blue just as 10/40 < 30/40.
  • There 15/40 blue vs. 25/40 red suggests red is more plausible than blue just as 15/40 < 25/40, but our certainty dropped because 25/40 < 30/40.
  • There 19/40 blue vs. 21/40 red suggests red is just slightly more plausible than blue just as 19/40 = 0.475 < 0.525 = 21/40 but not by much.

A proportion of a set of events (X) is its size divided by the size of all events ()

In this story an event is a single ball being chosen. So (X)= “a red ball is chosen” is an event with 30 options in the first case, 25 in the second, and 21 in the last. Proportions are by design always between 0 and 1. A 0 proportion occurs when no events occur and a 1 when all events occur. And we can compare any subset of events in this way because any two subsets (X,Y) have a finite size.

Proportions are the first and main example of a probability, that is, an assignment of a number to an abductive reasoning problem. Let us set up the details.

  • Context (): Drawing balls from a bag with red and blue balls.
  • Result : (R) draw a red.
  • First Hypothesis: (H) There are more red balls than blue balls.
  • Second Hypothesis :(K) Random event.

We still could not truly know unless we list all the balls. There may not be enough time to do such a lengthy count. So instead we take our proportion as evidence of some form of growing belief that there may be more red than blue balls and our confidence in this has something to do with the numbers 3/4 and 1/4.
