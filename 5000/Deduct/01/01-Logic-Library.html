Deductive Reasoning

Choosing a Logic

Logical arguments play by rules, which is what makes people believe logical arguments. However, these rules change from context to context, much like the rules of a game. See for yourself.

Suppose you read the manual and find to facts:

  • “If you are an Administrator then you can add users.”
  • “If you are an Administrator then you can remove users.”

Logically, this is the same as the single sentence.

  • “If you are an Administrator then you can add and remove users.”

If you wanted to explain the reasoning you could give further examples, for instance replace (Administrator, Add User, Remove User) with (Teacher, Teach, Grade) or (Chef, Cook, Clean). To emphasize the logic we might also begin to strip away artifacts of language and leave the main content. \[ \begin{array}{rcl} \text{If Teacher} & \text{then} & \text{can Teach} \\ \text{If Teacher} & \text{then} & \text{can Grade} \\ \hline \text{If Teacher} & \text{then} & \text{can Teach and Grade} \end{array} \qquad \begin{array}{rcl} \text{Chef} & \to & \text{cook} \\ \text{Chef} & \to & \text{clean} \\ \hline \text{Chef} & \to & \text{cook and clean} \end{array} \] where \(\to\) reads as “implies” or “if … then …”. Mathematicians like to use \(\Rightarrow\) as well for that job but \(\to\) is the symbol used in logic and other sciences and our choice here too. Thinking like a mathematician you would soon give up on examples and replace their roles with variables \((A, B, C)\) and write the argument as \[ \tag{Rule of Implied Conjunction} \begin{array}{rcl} A & \to & B \\ A & \to & C \\ \hline A & \to & B \text{ and } C. \end{array} \] Congratulations, you have just found a rule of “formal logic”, formal here refers to “form”, i.e. shape of the argument, not that the argument needs cloth napkins and a top hat.

Now let us keep the form (shape) of the argument but change the topic. Notice your belief in this form of reasoning quickly vanishes.

On a laptop:

  • “If you have a USB port then you can connect a mouse.”
  • “If you have a USB port then you can connect a keyboard.”

However,

  • “It may not be true that if you have a USB port you can connect a mouse and a keyboard.”

Obviously you need two USB ports to connect both, and the hypothesis is only clear about having one.

It is striking that most human reasoning knows intuitively not to use the form of argument we used on example 1 with example 2. We could replicate the same experiment and replace (USB port, Mouse, Keyboard) with (Egg, Cookies, Brownies) or variaables (A, B, C) and had we begun with this example we would have instead derived a fallacy instead of a rule! \[ \tag{Fallacy of Implied Conjunction} \begin{array}{rcl} A & \to & B \\ A & \to & C \\ \hline A & \not\to & B \text{ and } C. \end{array} \] Note that in notation, a “strike through” such as \(\not \to\) (or \(\not\Rightarrow\)) of a symbol means it is not true, so the fallacy is that \(A\) does not lead to \(B\) and \(C\).

So is logic to be believed or not?

What we believe as logical depends very much on context. The rule of conjunction made sense for the topic of being an administrator but was fallacy of conjunction when dealing with resources. Both are used correctly in everyday reasoning but they seem to say the exact opposite beliefs. This is the entire point of this article: to make a convincing argument is to play by rules within an agreed to context. Like the rules of a video game which are not always the same from logic to logic. Each logic is a universe of its own. Play with enough logics and you might be quicker to pick up rules of a new one and you will also start to appreciate what it is you really like about the rules of your favorite game, I mean, logic.

NotePython

Python is a programming platform created originally by Guido van Rossum named for a British comedy group, Monty Python. It’s strengths are broad adoption, huge community of users, and breadth of packages. Its drawbacks are limited support to catch mistakes and opportunities to miss use data. Python reads the task as a command to concatenate (glue together) the two lists into one. This is a versatile operation and you could imagine using it to make a lot of useful programs. That is why Python makes that choice in its logic.

NoteJulia

Berzanson et. al.’s Julia observes the logic of adding the internal numbers, what is known as “vector addition”. That understanding tracks with centuries of mathematical and scientific uses. So there is a strong case to want that within programs instead. While we are hear lets mention that Julia’s strengths are speed and scientific computing libraries. Its drawbacks are harder syntax and that it is not python.

What does Artificial Intelligence think?

Steady improvements in Large Language Models (LLMs) (an example of Artificial Intelligence AI) have made it possible to skip over the many artificial languages of the past few decades. The strength of LLMs is that they instructed in natural language (much like algorithms of the first 4950 years of computing) and they link up vast amounts of information. The downsides include failure to follow instructions and expensive computing.

Sometimes the rules of logic change even between individuals or groups, what we might call house rules. A mathematician might insist \(0\) is not a natural number while a computer scientist insists that it is. As you personalize your use of AI it may give a host of unwritten house rules and it may become confusing when sharing your results with others. So it matters to learn a uniform vocabulary at least so that we can extract a result that can be shared with others. And just as with games, it is ok to have favorites and others you find tedious.

Think of a game you prefer to play with house rules that are different from the rules on the box. How do your rules modify the game play? Do they make it fast, slower, easier, more challenging?
