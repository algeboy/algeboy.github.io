Curry-Howard-Lambek Correspondence

Recall we opened these articles with a discussion of dividing 541 tiles into rows of 101 tiles each. This lead us to uncover a relationship between proofs, programs, and diagrams. In this article we will switch out the use of precise numbers and start to work towards a more precise translation between the sorts of proofs, programs, and diagrams we see with a simple division algorithm.

Division as math

Perhaps the most important point of the experiment was to clarify the aim. Notice that we ended with two claims that both had to be true at once. The first was that \(541=5\cdot 101 + 36\), which reflects the idea that we keep track of all the tiles. The second was that \(36 < 101\) which reflected the goal of tiling as many rows as possible. One of the most important lessons in solving a problem is to actually state clearly what problem you aim to solve. Let us do that now, but with \(m\) tiles placed in rows of \(n\) tiles each.

Claim. When counting with natural numbers \(0,1,2,...\), if we are given \(m\) and \(n>0\), then there exists natural numbers \(q\) and \(r\) such that \[ m = qn + r \text{ and } r < n. \]

Notice the mathematics has forced us to express many unsaid but important facts. We only want to talk about counting numbers, what mathematics calls natural numbers. So we won’t be seeing a remainder of say \(2.3\) or \(\sqrt{\pi}\). Second, we have separated clearly the result from the method used to justify it. There is no discussion here of the steps we performed. So why should anyone believe our claim? To convince them we next include what mathematics calls a proof, an argument that convinces our audience.

Proof. We proceed by considering the number of tiles \(m\) and the number of tiles per row \(n\). We repeatedly subtract \(n\) from \(m\) until the remaining number of tiles is less than \(n\). The number of times we subtracted \(n\) is the quotient \(q\), and the remaining number of tiles is the remainder \(r\). By construction, we have \[ m = qn + r \text{ and } r < n, \] which completes the proof.

If we now stand back and think of \(m\) tiles stretched in rows of length \(n\) we can see this method will find \(q\) rows with a remainder \(r<n\) of left over or rejected tiles where \[ m=qn+r\text{ and }r<n. \]

This is the a complete solution but the specifics were never the point. Lets inspect how we solved it. Perhaps the most important point was to know what we accomplished. Notice when we concluded we had two terms: the quotient \(q\) and the remainder \(r\). Saying those words meant \(541 = 5\cdot 101 + 36\) and \(36 < 101\). What we did was prove this

There were two phases. One was a step in which we were forced to stop, when the tiles remaining are too few. We call this a base case. The second situation, called a recursive case, is when we have enough tiles to complete a full row. We take that away from the tiles we had and start the problem all over with new numbers. This is programming strategy called a recursion. In fact the specific mix of recursion here is so special it has a nickname called a loop.

Many a programmer has been helped by seeing this in a diagram known to many as a flow-chart, though others have suggested the name string diagram. We read a flow chart as system of pipes that moves the data from each node to the next in the direction of the arrows. Each node does something to the data as described inside the label. A label on an edge acts like a filter. So if the data does not match the filter then you cannot move your data through that arrow. The name “loop” may make more sense when we review the shape of the flow-chart.

Remark. A flow-chart need not be thought of as picture. In fact the picture is intentionally meant to allow for a lot of variability. You can move a node around for example without interrupting the flow. So the flow-chart is actually the collection of relationships and their labels. So it is a language which might be written in words, spoken allowed, or drawn in pictures.

For example, in the second table we record the same flow-chart by list in words and symbols all the nodes, arrows, and labels. For instance Start[q=0, r=m] represents a starting node with label “q=0, r=m”. In another line Ask -->|True| Yes represents an arrow from the “Ask” node to the “Yes” node with the label “True”.

Our examples includes a few technical terms like flowchart TD and is wrapped by the symbols ```. Learning a graphical language like this can be useful when changing designs as well as in sharing concepts with anyone who cannot see your screen. It is also enormously helpful when inputting a diagram to a chatbot or AI assistant.

Division as software

To wrap up, we saw three ways to think of division:

  • A Proof: an argument to confirm the division is possible.
  • A Program: as a list of steps to carry out division.
  • A Diagram: a language (often visual) to express division in steps.

Each way to think is as good as the next but each seems to emphasize our understanding differently.

Memoization

Now that we have an algorithm to divide we should ask what were the Babylonian clay tables, for since it did not appear in anything we just described.

You might discover that you get faster if you can build on what you already have computed before. For example, suppose the day before you had already laid 440 tiles 101 tiles wide and you left your self a memo, reminding you that this wall stood 4 rows high with 36 remaining tiles. Together that means: \[ 440 = 4\cdot 101 + 36 \quad \text{and} \quad 36<101. \] So you from your memo you could work back to today’s problem by adding back the 101 for the first row you placed down to get: \[ \begin{aligned} 541 & = 101+440 = 101 + 4\cdot 101 + 36, & 36 \lt 101\\ 541 & = 5\cdot 101 + 36, & 36 \lt 101. \end{aligned} \] So we can tile 5 rows high with 36 tiles remaining. The real savings here is the ability wall off (:eye_roll:) steps and solve them independently. That way if you have more than one way to solve it you can simply choose the solution you find most efficient. By invoking the previous day’s lesson of dividing \(440\) tiles by \(101\), we save time and effort in solving the current problem.

If we wanted to emphasize the steps instead of the outcomes we could put these in a diagram where events lead from one state to another. State here is a term used to mean where everything is, for example, “my house is in such a messy state” is a way to saying the items in your home our not where they belong. The state in our division problem refers to all our numbers and equations.

The Babylonian clay tablets are their memos for division. To use them you first subtract the denominator from your numerator until you find the ratio on the clay table memo which has recorded with it the division result for that pair. You then take that known result and add back what you subtracted to get your answer. In our example \(541/101\) was reduced to \(440/101\) which had a memo \(4+36/101\). As we subtracted only \(1\) denominator we now add it back to get \(541/101=1+4+36/101=5+36/101\).

Programming where you save facts as memos to speed up future work is called memoization, closely related to memorization though the latter is the word that captures all the different tricks, mnemonics, and repetition techniques humans use to recall information.

If you followed all of this then you indeed know how to divide with remainder. What matters now is to explain this without specific numbers but in preparation for any future possible choices. As simple as that sounds we might find doing it forces is to be much more explicit about what some of the ideas of tiling really meant. Let us go through this in three different ways.

Division in general

We now tie the two strategies together by considering both cases.

  • Proof
  • Commutative Diagram
  • Program

Claim. For any non-negative integers \(m\) and \(n\) with \(n>0\), there are integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that \(m=qn+r\) and \(0 \le r < n\).

Proof. If \(m\lt n\) then

\(m=0\cdot n+m \lt n\); so, use \(q=0\) and \(r=m\).

Otherwise,

Take \(k=m-n\). As \(n>0\), it follows that \(m\gt k\). Hence, division of \(k\) by \(n\) is a smaller problem which we assume was previously solved. So take \(k=q\cdot n+r\) with \(r\lt n\). Add \(n\) to both sides and factor: \[ m = n+ k = n+qn + r = (1+q)n + r \] So \(1+q\) and \(r\) solve the division problem for \(m\) and \(n\).

\(\Box\)

In this complex diagram any two paths with the same start and end points represent equal results. We call this a commutative diagram. Like a bike ride from you home to the office, you can take different paths but the commutes are the same.

# Division Algorithm as a Python program
def div(m, n):
    if m < n:
        return 0, m
    else:
        k = m - n
        q, r = div(k, n)
        return 1+q, r
 
# Random test of the left side
m = random.randint(0, 100);  j = random.randint(0, 100)
n = m+j+1
@assert div(m,n) == (0, m)
 
# Random test of the right side
n = random.randint(0, 100);  k = random.randint(0, 100)
m = n + k
q, r = div(n, n)
@assert div(n+k, n) == (1+q, r)

What is the point?

You must be asking yourself how reading about division using a tiles is going to prepare you to work with Artificial Intelligence, Data Science, or anything else modern.

First a social point. Babylonians were a society that built giant pyramids called ziggurats, fountains, gardens, predicted eclipses well into the future, and yes tiled marvelous gates still on display today. So having division on the mind is not a sign of intellectual weakness. Often difficult concepts arise amongst simpler ones and offer a less threatening path to get to the hard stuff. We will touch on many state-of-the-art topics in mathematics, computation, and data science. Yet, I will prefer simple setups so that anyone can participate. You never know when you might need to explain what you are planning to a client, a boss, a coworker, a loved one who wants to connect with what you do, or a young person learning from you. These stories are for them.

Now for the scientific merit. We are free to interpret division as a program, perhaps to lay tiles, but also repurposed to for any use of a quotient and remainder. At the same time it was a proof that natural numbers \(m\) and \(n\neq 0\) can always be related by \(m=q\cdot n+r\) with \(0\le r<n\). Lastly there was a diagram which served as a language to often more precise than natural speech and writing, to present division and the composition of a bunch of smaller believable claims or steps. So division, as presented, is equal parts a program, a proof, and a logic. Division is not alone in this relationship.

Curry-Howard-Lambek Correspondence. Precise proofs, working programs, and commutative diagrams are interchangeable.

These articles are an exploitation of this correspondence. Sometimes a proof will seem more obvious than its program. Other times a program is where we start. Often there is a diagram that explains both better. That logic does not see these as different just means you are entitled to use anyone of them to get started and transfer the impacts to the others. When we are lucky the correspondences go back-and-forth in reinforcing cycles.
