Syntax and Semantics

Language of “And”

The best tool language has for clarifying meaning is the use of a grammar. If we keep to a context-free grammar we can be certain that everyone reads the meaning the same. So here is a grammar we can give to “and” to fit our logical purposes. Note, in historical settings the term “conjunction” is often used as synonym for “and”. Using “conjunction” can often solve the annoying problem needing to use “and” as an every day word while also talking about “and” in a logical way.

In symbolic language \(\wedge\) (“wedge” but pronounced “and”) is used to represent the logical operator “and”. The grammar for “and” is given by the following production rule: \[ \tag{$L_{\wedge}$} \langle conjunction \rangle ::= \langle proposition \rangle \wedge \langle proposition \rangle \] In natural language we can say “A and B” where A and B are propositions. Alternatives include &, && and AND but we will stick to \(\wedge\) for the rest of this book.

Notice what we have not allowed are some the natural ways that “and” drifts in language, e.g. 

“7 is positive, odd and prime”

would now need to be said more like this:

“(7 is positive) and ((7 is odd) and (7 is prime))”

We forgive most authors who slide back to the more natural language uses of “and” but it will strengthen your understanding to practice this in a precise way for a while.

In a theoretical judgment where we forget about evidence then all we need from propositions is their truth value. We can ask for this in most programming languages by builtin logical operators that are written in a variety of ways. Here are some popular options.

  • p && q is used in C, C++, Java, JavaScript, and R.
  • p and q is used in Python, Ruby, and Pascal.
  • (and p q) is used in Lisp and Scheme.
  • p ∧ q in Agda

Note that these result in a truth value only, they do not hold on to any evidence.

In a computational judgment we need data (evidence) that proves the propositions. So a proposition \(P\) has data \(x\) that proves it, and a proposition \(Q\) has data \(y\) that proves it. We combine these as a joint proposition

  • (P,Q) in Scala
  • Pair<P,Q> in Java.
  • tuple[P,Q] in Python.
  • std::pair<P,Q> in C++.
  • P × Q in Agda.

These are just the propositions, they will need data to be proved.

In a practical judgment of a proposition \(P\) we associate some value \(\varepsilon\) of truth, for example, a percentage of our believe the \(P\) is true. If we have similar proposition \(Q\) with a value \(\delta\) then to judge \(P\wedge Q\) we need to combine these values. Some of the options include

  • min(ε,δ) assumes whichever the lower of two percentages should be the default.
  • ε*δ assumes the two percentages are independent and so the product is a reasonable estimate of the joint probability.

Introduction Rule for conjunction

Now get into what drives the meaning of “and” by describing what must be present to ensure we can create an instance of its use. Logicians call these introduction rules because they introduce the symbols of the logical operator, in this case \(\wedge\).

In natural language we use “and” when we combine two valid propositions \(P\) and \(Q\) to make a new valid proposition \(P\wedge Q\).

Now in symbolic language to say a proposition is valid is to have judged it in a context. So we start with \[ \Gamma \vdash P \quad \Delta \vdash Q \] for contexts \(\Gamma\) and \(\Delta\). We conclude \[ \Gamma, \Delta \vdash P \wedge Q \]

The mix of words and symbols is helpful initially but when we start to work with logic it becomes much more of a calculation, a calculus. Because this type of calculus is a movement of sequences it is known as sequent calculus. In the full sequent calculus we can collapse the words to this: \[ \tag{$I_{\wedge}$} \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma &\vdash P\\ \Delta & \vdash Q\\ \hline \Gamma, \Delta & \vdash P \wedge Q \end{array} \]

If we think of a program as making verified judgments then it makes sense that a proposition \(P\) has data \(x\) the proved it, and a proposition \(Q\) has data \(y\) that proved it. So to create data the proves both we can just hold on to both \(x\) and \(y\). The notation for this is an ordered pair \((x,y)\). The notations vary between programming languages, here are some popular options. Assuming x and y are already data:

  • p=(x,y) in Python, Ruby, and JavaScript.
  • (define p (list x y)) in Lisp and Scheme.
  • Pair p = new Pair(x,y) in Java.
  • p = <x,y> in Magma.
  • auto p = std::make_pair(x, y); in C++.

Elimination Rule for conjunction

Once we have conjoined two propositions we can carry them around as a single proposition until we intend to use it. It is at this point that we want the ability reach back and extract the to original propositions. Logicians call these elimination rules because they eliminate the symbols of the logical operator, in this case \(\wedge\). Something new happens with this step because it not one but two uses we expect. So technically we are after two elimination rules.

In natural language we use \(P\wedge Q\) so that we can know \(P\) or that we can know \(Q\), or both. So we need to change from a context that judged \(P\wedge Q\) to a context that judges \(P\) and a context that judges \(Q\). In symbolic language we can write this as two elimination rules: \[ \frac{ \Gamma\vdash P\wedge Q }{ \Gamma \vdash P }(E_{-\wedge *}) \qquad \frac{ \Gamma\vdash P\wedge Q }{ \Gamma \vdash Q }(E_{*\wedge -}) \]

when we have a reason \(R\) to know of a proposition \(P\wedge Q\) given to us in a context \(\Gamma\). Then we can say that \(R\to P\) represents that the reason \(R\) leads to \(P\) and \(R\to Q\) represents that the reason \(R\) leads to \(Q\). In diagram language we could say \(R\to (P\wedge Q)\) represents that the reason \(R\) leads to both \(P\) and \(Q\).

Finally in symbolic language this would be the following rule to add to our proof tree options.

  • p[0] in Python, Ruby, and JavaScript.
  • (car p) for first, (cdr p) for rest, in Lisp and Scheme.
  • p.fst or x where t=(x y) in Haskell.
  • p.first in C++.
  • p.1 in Magma.

Let \(p\otimes q\) be a logical operator that is like \(\wedge\) but it is a resource operator. That is, if we have \(p\otimes q\) then we can use \(p\) or \(q\) but not both. Write down the LIE for this operator.

Clarity through Syntax

In order to be extra clear about our logic we want to set aside the words in natural language an use instead symbols or words in special fonts so as to treat the rules with some rigor that cannot miscommunicate out meaning. To so this we start to make a system that resembles arithmetic with is various additions, subtractions and other operators. When we calculate with any symbols we get what is known as a “Calculus”

So far we have seen \(\lambda\)-calculus and now we will be looking at two further Calculi, Propositional and Sequent. This is as good a place as any to pause and mention the list of “calculi” that exist in this course.

As a cultural warning, when some says they are studying “Calculus” chances are they mean one of the first two. Our main objective is to start making logical operators and we will focus on “AND”. In assignments you will explore “OR” and “NOT”

Sequents

Sequents means “parts of as a sequence” and the entire idea is to separate the premise from a conclusion. The devices to separate can just be a line, a long space, a word like “entails” but the most common one today is the turnstyle ⊢also said “vdash” since that is how you look it up as a font.

Sequents calculus is the use of sequence to calculate truth in a specified logic. If our logic only has “and” we can only calculate how make an and (introduction) and how to apply an and (elimination). All this must be written in a language.

  • (L)anguage : the alphabat, symbols, and grammar to be used in the sequents. If your sequents do not parse in the grammar then they don’t count.
  • (I)ntroduction : what premise or premises do you need to get ot use your logical operator?
  • (E)limination : what premises along with context do you need to use a logical operator?

The LIE components of a logical operator are a good way to remember the steps.

Semantics

There is another way we can describe logical operators that get closer to our appreciation of what our data means. For example, 12 is even and positive is true because each of “12 is even” and “12 is positive” are true. If this is all we care about we can ignore the precise form in which we get the facts and focus purely on the values. This is known as “Semantic” reasoning.

When it comes the semantics of AND we insist on one rule:

Only True AND True should be True

However this still leaves us lots of room to explore meaning.

Classical Theoretical Logic

In classical logic, what experts call “KL” (the German initials) we insist every event is judged as true \(\top\) or false \(\bot\) with no middle values allowed (that rule is called the “Law of the Excluded Middle (LEM)”). That means that \(P\wedge Q\) can take only 4 combinations.

\(P\) \(Q\) \(P\wedge Q\)
\(\top\) \(\top\) \(\top\)
\(\top\) \(\bot\) \(\bot\)
\(\bot\) \(\top\) \(\bot\)
\(\bot\) \(\bot\) \(\bot\)

Many times this information is recorded more compactly as a table where \(P\) and \(Q\) label the rows and columns

\[ \begin{array}{|c|cc|} \hline \wedge & \top & \bot \\ \hline \top & \top & \bot \\ \bot & \bot & \bot\\ \hline \end{array} \] In fact when computing we often replace the symbols with 0 \(\bot\) and 1 \(\top\) and notice this is the multiplication rules of these numbers

\[ \begin{array}{|c|cc|} \hline * & 1 & 0 \\ \hline 1& 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\\ \hline \end{array} \]

Notice we could be using very different algorithms to calculate AND through semantics. We might be looking things up in a table or multiplying integers, or using transistors in special configuration called a logic-gate. The point is, if we look at AND as is values instead of its syntax we have the freedom to engineer how we actually apply AND. That can be a powerful feature.

Many valued logical AND

Finally, much of life is not so certain and while we may describe some things as true \(\top\) we might struggle to conclude that all other things are false \(\bot\). For that many logics have been created that use further values. Sometimes it is a U for “Unknown”. Some times a “M” for mixed” (maybe a concept that has elements of truth and falsity), or “F(x)” for a “fuzzy truth $0x$” to measure quantities that vary continuously.

I like to use the symbol \(+\) because it seems typographically between \(\top\) and \(\bot\) and reminds me we could slide the bar up or down to introduce many subdivisions of “truth”. Obeying the rule of and: ONLY TRUE AND TRUE IS TRUE, we are still permitted to assign the remains values in different ways. For example, perhaps \(\top \wedge +\) should be \(+\), meaning, if you have something true and something partially true, then the whole thing is partially true. Of course context gets to decide what will count as the new meaning of AND. We close by detailing one of the ANDs as defined by the logician Kleene.

\[ \begin{array}{|c|ccc|} \hline \wedge & \top & + & \bot \\ \hline \top & \top & + & \bot \\ + & + & + & \bot \\ \bot & \bot & \bot & \bot\\ \hline \end{array} \]

In words: truth and truth is true; truth and partial truth is partially true but any value and a falsity is false. There are clearly many ways that this captures the mode of a logic for AND even when we cannot be certain that our data is good enough to make things exactly true or false. And to make this semantics into arithmetic, suppose we used 1 for \(\top\), 0 for \(+\), and -1 of \(\bot\). Then \(P\wedge Q\) becomes merely \(\min(P,Q)\). For example \(\min(1,0)=0\) which is what it means to have True and Partially true being only Partially true.

As you develop your logic you may have many other ways to approach operators like this one. But resist the urge to call it an AND unless it obey the main rule: Only True and True is True.
