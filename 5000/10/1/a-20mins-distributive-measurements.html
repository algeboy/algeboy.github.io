10.1.a (20mins): Distributive Measurements

** Measurements

In a ruler the whole is the sum of its parts… or is it? Sometimes illusions deserve attention.

*An illusion with a Lesson

There is an illusion so convincing it has earned its own Wikipedia article. It takes a triangle, cuts it into polygons, and rearranges them. Yet once re-arranged, the parts no longer cover the original area! These diagrams of curtesy of Original: CC-BY Krauss. 

Does the surprise you? While this is an illusion (explained below) the reason it works as a brain teaser is that we are conditioned to understand area does not get lost amongst the parts. Our mind hunts for an explanation. This should not be possible, we believe.

Explanation of the puzzle

The main clue to offer to the illusion is to point out that the first triangle is not actually a triangle, but a pentagon. So the lower triangle has slightly different angles than the upper one. So put together they do not make one large triangle but a polygon. You can detect this better if we exaggerate the difference in angle.

So what appears as a hypotenuse of a large triangle is in fact dented, and thus looses a small amount of area which our eyes fail to detect. When we rearrange the parts we do not see the an area disappear. In fact the opposite. By rearranging the parts we gather the missing area form the dent into one place where its far more noticeable. Area, as we think of it, truly is the sum of its parts.

*Integral measurements: The details and Examples

So as seen in the video the world around us has dozens of natural way to glue data together. Whether that be a sum like (x+y) or a disjoint union (XY), addition of resistance, pressure, sales prices and so much more. These quite often behave in just the way the need for the whole to be the sum of its parts. And it introduces an opportunity to use common stories of length and area as metaphors for the often complicated seeming calculations we find ourselves doing. So for the next three parts we explore this technology. We begin by formally declare what is a measurement.

Example 2**: Lebesgue’s Area Measure

The concept of distance generalizes to area, and then volumes. Obviously the measure of a rectangle is width times height.

If we expect a measure then the total shaded area is the sum of the disjoint shaded areas, i.e. our square (I_1) in blue on the left and our ellipse (I_2) on the right. We will call this the Lebesque measure as well, only now in 2-D. So what is this measure? The shaded rectangle is partitioned into 9 blue sub-squares. Warning: When don’t we have a measure? Suppose instead of area we looked at the picture above and asked to measure “how tall is the shaded region?”

  • Option 1. Suppose we understand this sentence as asking how many boxes high is enough to fit both pictures, and we can count the blue box as 3 high and the ellipse as 2. So 3 is enough. If we did this we would be answering the question correctly but also demonstrating we do not have a measure. That is the whole has measure 3, and the parts have measures 3 and 2 but (3+2). If we really wanted to include this as a measure then we would need to revisit the idea that the two shapes are disjoint. While they are disjoint in the xy-plane they are not disjoint in the y direction alone. And so in that sense this is not a violation of measurement rules, just a misunderstanding about the meaning of disjoint for this case. An “illusion” as we have been warned about already.
  • Option 2. An alternative reading of this question is to ask if we stack up the shapes how tall would they be? Here we would in fact add 3 to 2 to get 5. This would be a measure as the whole would be the sum of the parts.

It is crucially important to be precise in our language of measurement. Many times a miscommunication or imprecise phrasing can lead to errors in measurement.

What else can be measured?

Geometry offered our first measures as line segments. Probability took us further letting use measure points that might spread out but still have a clear notion of measurement. To everyone’s surprise, in 1970 Robert Solovay discovered a logical context (by making an additional Set Theory axiom about so-called “Large Cardinals”) where every subset of decimal numbers is measurable! Not many people today are versed in those methods so it’s not a practical solution to Galileo’s dream, but the fact that this is possible proves that if you really need to measure a shape, you probably can, but perhaps with a lot of work!
