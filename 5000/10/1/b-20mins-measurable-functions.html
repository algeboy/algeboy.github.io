** Measurable Functions & Measure Zero

To set up new measures we need functions that care about measurement. These unimaginatively are called “measurable functions”.

*Comparing Measures with Functions

Since we can have lots of ways to measure it will be enormously helpful to have ways to relate different measurements. To get a feel lets begin by consider the interval Lebsgue measure ().

Wherever the tub intersects our graph we can turn the intersection vertically and draw vertical red shaded region that eventually intersects the input (x)-axis at a union of intervals.

Let’s summarize what we are seeing. We are measuring intervals and the function we plotted had the effect that if we could measure the output (as interval in the codomain) then we can trace that back to an input that was in fact also measurable (a union of intervals). A function that can relate output measurements to input measurements is one that we can reason about in predictable ways (more precise details on that below). So this is a property of functions that is worth observing and naming. We call these “measurable functions”. And in keeping with the anticipated many uses of measurement in your life this not only a concept for intervals. We can use it to compare all sorts of measures.

General properties

  • Measurable functions are stable under composition: If and are both measurable then so is their composition .
  • Measurable functions are stable under approximations: Limits of measurable functions are measurable.
  • Measurable functions (f:) split into an all positive measure (f^+:) and an all negative part (f^-:) where (f=f^+ + f^-).
  • Peicewise continuous functions (f:) are “Lebesgue measurable”: intervals in the output come from union of intervals in the input.

General measurable functions in probability and statistics

The measurable functions on probability spaces are so essential to the theory of probability and statistics that they have developed their own vocabulary and notation.

Random Variables

Given a probability measure (also called a probability distribution) on outcomes , the measurable sets are called events and the measurable functions are called random variables.

Don’t confuse “Random Variables” with function that give the probability of specific events. They are not these at all. Rather they are way to express and combine descriptions of events we like to explore within a fixed probability. Just like we can describe a die roll without knowing if we have a fair die, we can describe random variables without knowing the probability of events. It is the combination of probability with random variables that makes for a potent measurement. This will evolve later in this module.
