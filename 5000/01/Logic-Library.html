3  Choosing a Logic

Logical arguments play by rules, which is what makes believe them. However, these rules change from context to context, much like the rules of a game. See for yourself.

If your manual says:

  • “If you are an Administrator then you can add users.”
  • “If you are an Administrator then you can remove users.”

Logically, this is the same as the single sentence.

  • “If you are an Administrator then you can add and remove users.”

If you wanted to explain the reasoning you could give further examples, for instance replace (Administrator, Add User, Remove User) with (Teacher, Teach, Grade) or (Chef, Cook, Clean). To emphasize the logic we might also begin to strip away artifacts of language and leave the main content. \[ \begin{array}{rcl} \text{If Teacher} & \text{then} & \text{can Teach} \\ \text{If Teacher} & \text{then} & \text{can Grade} \\ \hline \text{If Teacher} & \text{then} & \text{can Teach and Grade} \end{array} \qquad \begin{array}{rl} \text{Chef} & \to & \text{cook} \\ \text{Chef} & \to & \text{clean} \\ \hline \text{Chef} & \to & \text{cook and clean} \end{array} \] where \(\to\) reads as “implies” or “if … then …”. Thinking like a mathematician you would soon give up on examples and replace their roles with variables \((A, B, C)\) and write the argument as \[ \tag{Rule of Implied Conjunction} \begin{array}{rcl} A & \to & B \\ A & \to & C \\ \hline A & \to & B \text{ and } C. \end{array} \] Congratulations, you have just found a rule of “formal logic”, formal here refers to “form”, i.e. shape of the argument, not that the argument needs cloth napkins.

Now let us keep the form (shape) of the argument but change the topic. Notice your belief in this form of reasoning quickly vanishes.

On a laptop:

  • “If you have a USB port then you can connect a mouse.”
  • “If you have a USB port then you can connect a keyboard.”

However,

  • “It may not be true that if you have a USB port you can connect a mouse and a keyboard.”

Obviously you need two USB ports to connect both, and the hypothesis is only clear about having one.

It is striking that most human reasoning knows intuitively not to use the form of argument we used on example 1 with example 2. We could replicate the same experiment and replace (USB port, Mouse, Keyboard) with (Egg, Cookies, Brownies) or variaables (A, B, C) and had we begun with this example we would have instead derived a fallacy instead of a rule! \[ \tag{Fallacy of Implied Conjunction} \begin{array}{rcl} A & \to & B \\ A & \to & C \\ \hline A & \not\to & B \text{ and } C. \end{array} \] Note that in notation, a “strike through” of a symbol means it is not true, so the fallacy is that \(A\) does not lead to \(B\) and \(C\).

So what should we believe as logical, the rule of conjunction or the fallacy of conjunction? Both are used correctly in everyday reasoning but they seem to say the exact opposite beliefs. This is the entire point of this article: to make a convincing argument is to play by rules within an agreed to context. Like the rules of a video game which are not always the same from logic to logic. Each logic is a universe of its own. Play with enough logics and you might be quicker to pick up rules of a new one and you will also start to appreciate what it is you really like about the rules of your favorite game, I mean, logic.

Makes up a scenarios that the Rule of Implied Conjunction is true and another scenario where the Fallacy of Implied Conjunction is true. What are the differences between the two scenarios?

So there is a strong case to want that within programs instead. While we are hear lets mention that Julia’s strengths are speed and scientific computing libraries. Its drawbacks are harder syntax and that it is not python.

The downsides include failure to follow instructions and expensive computing.

Sometimes the rules of logic change even between individuals or groups, what we might call house rules. A mathematician might insist \(0\) is not a natural number while a computer scientist insists that it is. As you personalize your use of AI it may give a host of unwritten house rules and it may become confusing when sharing your results with others. So it matters to learn a uniform vocabulary at least so that we can extract a result that can be shared with others. And just as with games, it is ok to have favorites and others you find tedious.

3.2 Deductive, Inductive, and Abductive reasoning

Once we settle on a form of judgment, be it computational \(\vdash\), practical \(\Vdash\), or theoretical \(\vDash\), we now have to ask what we want to judge. It was here that the logician Charles Sanders Peirce (1839-1914) laid out a spectrum.

  • Deductive Reasoning: to judge from the evidence.
  • Inductive Reasoning: to judge beyond the evidence.
  • Abductive Reasoning: to find the best hypothesis for the evidence.

These we will unpack in later hours, with the first four hours concentrating on deductive reasoning, the next four on inductive reasoning, and the last four on abductive reasoning.

4 Summary

  • Judgments are assigning truth values to proposotions.
  • Flavors of judgment include computational, practical, and theoretical.
  • The styles of judgement are organized around deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning.
