5  Logical Judgments

When we speak of reasoning we often ground our language in terms about “truth”. Philosophers still debate what this term should mean and obviously context helps considerably. We pickup the story after the philosophers. That is, we want to begin with the assumption that the meaning of truth and its possible values is already told to us and we now want to explore what we can do with that knowledge. Now it may seem the values of truth are obvious: “true” and “false”. Certainly that is the attitude of much of mathematics. Yet, that is a simplification that was rejected almost as soon as it was suggested.

A proposition is a statement that can be assigned a truth value. When it is assigned such a value we call that a judgement.

5.1 Boole’s True/False Judgments

A judgement is Boolean if it is either “true” (\(\top\)) or “false” (\(\bot\)). The name is in honor of George Boole (1815-1864) who first demonstrated that logic could be studied in part as a form of arithmetic on two values. The Boolean values are often denoted as \(\mathsf{Prop}=\{\top, \bot\}\).

Warning

Classical mathematics uses Boolean judgement which means that all proposition are explicitly true or false and never both. Since mathematics is uninterested in things that are false it makes no sense (other than learning) to include proposition that are false. As such the custom has emerged in mathematics to use the word “Proposition” only for those statements judged as true. This is a local custom of the field and propositions as used elsewhere are permitted to take on any truth value.

This book will take the more general meaning of this word preferring terms like “Theorem” for true propositions.

5.2 Popper’s Falsifiable Judgments

A judgement is Popperian if it can be falsified. The name is in honor of Karl Popper (1902-1994) who emphasized that experiments can rule out a theory but not actually prove it. This means that statements that have not been falsified are left with no judgement. The existence of a disproof can be written as a short flowchart \[ P\overset{x}{\longrightarrow }\bot\text{ that is, ``$x$ is evidence that $P$ leads to falsity''.} \] So \(\mathsf{Prop}=\{x:P\to \bot\}\) is the context of all statements \(P\) that could be judged as false. The purpose of a scientific experiment is to find such an \(x\) and thus falsify the proposition. If no such \(x\) can be found then we gain confidence that \(P\) might be true.

Note that the opposite of false is unknown which is not the same as true in Popperian judgement. Let us try out some Popperian judgments.

Existing Proposition \(P\): Heavy objects fall faster than light objects.

Experiment/Data \(x\):

  • Hypothesize \(P\)
  • Drop a bag of bricks (heavy object) and a bag of feathers (light object) from a tall building.
  • Observe heavier object does not fall faster than light object.
  • Conclude that assuming \(P\) leads to a falsehood, in symbols \(x: P\to \bot\).

Notice if we had not assumed \(P\) then experiment \(x\) would not have created anything false. It would just have been a piece of data. To make a falsehood requires we hypothesis a proposition is true and then use that truth in combination with our experiment or other judgments to arrive at something we judge to be false. The precise conditions will be described in the later chapter on implications. For now let us agree there is a common-sense understanding of this argument and a skeptics concerns will be addressed in the future.

Proposition \(P\): \(2^{2^n}+1\) is prime.

Use a Popperian judgment to value this proposition as false and to describe your evidence \(x:P\to \bot\).

In mathematics which tries to be Boolean we would take the false result of this exercise as evidence to claim it is a “false proposition”. Mathematicians call these counter examples of a proposition. Notice however that the Popperian judgement is evidence based and merely gives some limits on where the proposition is true. So often the more exciting questions that develop from Popperian judgments are to ask “Where is the proposition true?”

NoteFermat Primes Question

Are there infinitely many primes of the form \(2^{2^n}+1\)?

The similar question for Galileo is the unproven proposition:

Galileo’s Proposition \(Q\): All objects fall at the same speed (in a vacuum).

Notice there is no experiment to take can prove \(Q\) but every experiment that fails to falsify \(Q\) adds credence to the proposition. So Popperian judgment is one-sided.

5.3 Brouwer’s Verifiable Judgments

Somewhat the opposite to Popperian judgments are judgments in the philosophy of L. E. J. Brouwer.

A judgement is Brouwerian if it can be verified. The name is in honor of Luitzen Egbertus Jan Brouwer (1881-1966) who saw mathematics as the study of proof, not truth. He advocated a judgement where proofs are the values of propositions instead of true and false.

Similar to Popper the proof is the data in \(\mathsf{Prop}\), only now the idea is to begin with the assumption that there is at least one thing that is true (truth \(\top\) itself) and from that find evidence to transform that truth in to a proof of \(P\). So we swap from the flowchart \(P\to \bot\) as in Popper to flowchart \(\top\to P\). \[ \top\overset{x}{\longrightarrow } P\text{ which reads : ``$x$ is proof that truth leads to $P$''.} \] So \(\mathsf{Prop}=\{x:\top \to P\}\).

As with Popper, the opposite of true is unknown which is not the same as false. Today that style of judgment has found more uses in computation than in mathematics though the two are closely connected.

Let us compare a Boolean judgment with a Popperian and Brouwerian judgment.

Proposition \(P\): If \(n\) is an even natural number then \(n^2\) is also even.

In a Brouwerian judgment to claim this is true is to provide a proof.

NoteBrouwerian Judgment, i.e. Proof

To be even means we have evidence \(k\), a natural number, where \(n=2k\). From that we derive \[ n^2=(2k)^2=2(2k^2) \] So we produced evidence \(2k^2\) that \(n^2\) is even. So from a truth, \(n=2k\), we derived another truth \(P:\) \(n^2\) is even.

Meanwhile a Boolean judgement forget about process and just assert if something is true or false. Since we have already proved it we can now just declare it is true.

NoteBoolean Judgment, i.e. Value

\(P\) is true.

In particular we have the following relationship.

  • If a proposition has a Brouwerian judgment of true then it has true in Boolean judgement.
  • If a proposition has a Popperian judgment of false then it has false in Boolean judgement.

Note that a theoretical judgment does not necessarily mean that we know it’s truth value. It just means truth values exists and it does not depend on any state.

Proposition The number of times that \(p\) and \(p+2\) are both prime is infinite.

Boolean Value? Unknown!

So when we think of truth we often resort to propositions in Brouwerian judgment and convert them to Boolean truth. Likewise if we think a Boolean proposition should be false, we just need to create an example and use Popperian judgment to show it is false.

when we think of falsity we often resort to propositions in Popperian judgment. So if we want to prove something is false in Boolean judgment we just need to create an example, a model where the proposition is false. Because that means there can never be a Brouwerian proof of it being true. Similarly if we want to prove something is true in Boolean judgment we just need to create a Brouwerian proof of it being true. Because that means there can never be a Popperian example of it being false.

Both Popperian and Brouwerian judgments emphasize just one side of the truth table (false for Popper and true for Brouwer). This does not make them incapable of judgments the other way, for example they may exhaust all other possibilities, but usually requires some context or assumptions to understand that there is an exhaustible list of options. So these judgments leave open the possibility of having one-sided propositions.

5.4 Paraconsistent & Fuzzy Judgments

Engineers, lawyers and data scientist often work with judgments that are inconsistent. It could be that one sensor gives one reading and another a different reading,or a judge gets conflicting stories from witnesses, or that two copies of what are supposed to be the same data are just not equal. Have a judgement for that setting is crucial.

A judgment is Paraconsistent (“beyond consistent”) if it can be both true and false. These logics are older than Boolean but were rebuilt in with stricter structure in the 1900’s by several logicians includeing Newton da Costa (1929-2024), Stanisław Jaśkowski (1906-1965), and Lotfi Zadeh (1921-2017). There are many variations.

  • \(\mathsf{Prop}=\{\top, +, \bot\}\) (3-valued judgments of Kleene or Łukasiewicz) where \(+\) plays a role described with are “paradox” or “unknown” depending on the context.
  • \(\mathsf{Prop}=\{\top, \bot, \text{missing},\text{error}_1, \text{error}_2,\ldots\}\) where the extra values assume a role based on the definitions of an apparatus or programming language.
  • \(\mathsf{Prop}=\{ 0\leq \varepsilon\leq 1\}\) (fuzzy judgments) is a decimal value representing the degree of truth with \(1\) being absolutely true and \(0\) absolutely false. It is not uncommon to see the range shifted say to \(-1\leq \varepsilon\leq 1\) or \(-\infty\leq \varepsilon\leq \infty\). This shifts the arithmetic but not the intent of the judgment.

There are also variations which are one-sided such as discussing degrees of truth without degrees of falsity and vice-verse.

Proposition \(P\): A thermometer reading above a \(100^{\circ} F\) indicates a fever.

The thermometer is read three times with values \(99.2^{\circ} F\), \(100.4^{\circ} F\), and \(99.5^{\circ} F\).

  • In a three-valued logic we could judge \(P\) as \(+\) (both true and false) as it was true in one reading and false in others.
  • In a Fuzzy judgement we could assume there was a positive in 1/3 of the trials and report a 33% positive test.

6 Contextual Judgments: \(\vdash,\vDash,\Vdash\)

Now must judgments only make sense in a context. When this occurs it is necessary to include the context in the discussion and notation. Here we have some established options. Recall that we use \(\Gamma\) as a placeholder for a context. There are many styles of judgment beyond those we have explained here be they can roughly each be located under one of three categories.

  • Theoretical Judgments: assumes all judgments have a truth value and that value is trusted. Good for making models, predicting outcomes, finding limitations.
    • Main example: Boolean judgement, classical logic.
    • Terminology: \(\Gamma \vDash P\), “\(\Gamma\) is model for \(P\)” or “\(P\) holds in \(\Gamma\)”
  • Computational/Empirical Judgments: assumes judgments depend on evidence. Good for programming, proving correctness, finding bugs, and ruling out hypotheses/theories.
    • Main examples: Popperian and Brouwerian judgments, intuitionistic logic, resource aware logic, logic that considers time/space
    • Terminology: \(\Gamma \vdash P\), “\(\Gamma\) proves \(P\)” or “\(P\) is derivable from \(\Gamma\)”
  • Practical Judgments: assumes judgments may contain gaps and contradictions. Good for everyday reasoning and decision making especially when dealing with possibly messy data.
    • Main examples: Paraconsistent and Fuzzy judgments, modal logic for changing settings.
    • Terminology: \(\Gamma \Vdash P\), “\(\Gamma\) supports \(P\)” or “\(P\) is practically derivable from \(\Gamma\)”.

When ever we consider judgment of a general sort we will often resort to \(\vdash\) as both verifiable and falsifiable judgments are justifications in the theoretical and practical sense as well so this is the most conservative form of judgment we will consider.

For the most part the truth values of these three judgments will guide to the correct flavor so the following visualization, known as a Venn diagram, may help you draw the right connections.

  • \(\vdash\) Computational Logic
  • \(\vDash\) Theoretical Logic
  • \(\Vdash\) Practical Logic

7 Summary

  • Judgments assign truth values to propositions.
  • Judgment families include:
    • Theoretical (Boolean) for models and predictions.
    • Computational/Empirical (Popperian, Brouwerian) for programming and experiments.
    • Practical (Paraconsistent, Fuzzy) for real-world reasoning and decision making.
