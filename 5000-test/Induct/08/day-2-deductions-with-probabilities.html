Day 2: Deductions with Probabilities

Week 9 Day 2 Deductions with Probabilities

Our plan is to solve abductive problems using a connection to mathematical calculations by assigning probabilities to abductive settings. One idea is to make use of deductive logical operators as much as possible.

The Setup

Let () be our event/sample space and

  • (A={P()}) all the events satisfying a predicate (P(x)), and
  • (B={Q()}) all events satisfying some other predicate (Q(x)).

Then our goal is to look at the various logical operators between (P) and (Q) and formulate meaning as probabilities.

AND as Intersection of Events

Our first operator is a logical AND (). As (P(x)) and (Q(x)) becomes the event

So to assign a probability that models the deductive logic of AND is to use intersection of events.

(Prob(AB))

We cannot say precisely what formula to give to this probability as it is instead of the types of measurements that must be prescribed directly by the distribution of events. However, we can place an upper bound on the measurement because if an event occurs that is both of type A and of type B then it assumes both probabilities as a maximum.

(Prob(A)Prob(B) Prob(AB){Prob(A),Prob(B)})

When two events (A) and (B) are “independent” (why A occurs does not influence why B occurs) then we can in fact calculate with later formulas to show the lower bound happens in that case.

OR as Union with overlap

Our first operator is a logical OR (). As (P(x)) or (Q(x)) becomes the event

So to assign a probability that models the deductive logic of OR is to use union of events.

(Prob(AB))

We actually have a chance to use what we know to find a formula for this. As an opening guess if (A) and (B) have nothing in common, like two different numbers on a die, then the chances to get one event or the other is the sum of these two events:

[OR for independent events] (Prob(A B)=Prob(A)+Prob(B))

Example. (Independent OR)

If (A) is the event of rolling a 1 and (B) rolling a 2 then the event of rolling a 1 or a 2 is

(Prob(A B) =Prob(A) +Prob(B)= +=)

But before we stop here we should consider what it would mean to “Roll a 1 or 1”. Suddenly it does not make sense to consider this as 1/3, as there is still only one of the six faces of the cube with 1 pip. In fact, in any case where (A=B) we would then find

(Prob(AA)=Prob(A))

But our formula above would want (Prob(AA)=Prob(A)+Prob(A)=2Prob(A)). Something is wrong!

The resolution is that when we wrote the first formula we assumed that (A) and (B) are independent, have no event in common. This is not the case when (A=B) as there they have every event in common. So it seems our probability of A or B is over counting what they have in common, that is, they count what is in (AB) twice, once from (A) and once from (B). The solution then to dependent formula is as follows.

[OR for possibly dependent events] (Prob(A B)=Prob(A)+Prob(B)-Prob(AB))

Edge cases

There are two samples that are generally not interesting but allow us to setup the remaining calculations. These are

  • No event (={}).
  • All events ().

We assign these two specific probabilities.

  • (Prob()=0)
  • (Prob()=1)

It may seem funny to include the empty set as an option but sometimes this happens without intention. For example, reusing the illustration of cards, supposed we wanted to draw both a 2 and a 4. There is no card in standard deck that is both 2 and 4 and so we end up with the empty set:

(Prob( {} {})=Prob({})=0.)

Having a probability for empty sets is essential to guarantee that the arithmetic of our probability formulas is consistent.

NOT as complementary events

Next we consider the use of negation. Since (A={P()}), in a classical logic what is not true is false and we get a notion of a complementary set ({A}={P()}). To assign consistent probabilities to this behavior we should therefore seek to use a rule that turns the probability of ({A}) into whatever is not taken by (A).

(Prob({A})= 1 - Prob(A))

For example, if we roll a 6-sided die we expect the probability to get a 3 is 1/6. That means the odds against a 3 are calculated as

(Prob()=1-Prob(3)=1-=.)

Implication as Conditional Probablity

Perhaps the most important situation is that of modeling implication in probabilities. Let us recall the overall goal of abductive logic is to reason about which hypothesis (H) can lead us to a result (R) and to rank the hypotheses so we can know which to consider most plausible. In symbols this looks like this.

(, H R)

Now suppose for the sack of reasoning we turn this abductive questions into its own logical operator called “Conditional”

Conditional Operator

Recall our logical operators come in three parts: L.I.E. = Language, Introduction, Elimination.

Language. The language of conditional operator is typically (R|H) read as “R conditioned on H”, and H is called the “hypothesis”. The hypothesis may be nothing, in which case we call it simply the null hypothesis. If the null hypothesis is use it akin to say “R just happened without any explanation.” While we accept that sometimes “life happens” it is customary to ask if perhaps there was a reason for a result. So our goal in abductive reasoning is often to do better than the null hypothesis.

It may look therefore as if (RH) and (HR) achieve the same overall logic. After all, if we use conditional and arrive at (HR) then we can use one of the two elimination rules of AND to get (HRR) thus recovering the same result as implication. However, the subtle and important difference is that the reverse is not possible in some cases, at least not without limiting the types of logic we can use. For if we use a logic that counts resources it can be the case that the use of (H) in the premise of elimination (HR) could consume (H) meaning there is no further copy of (H) we can use to recombine with (R) and derive from it (HR). This is in fact how many games actually evolve. If you have a certain card (H) you might be able to exchange it for another card (R) but you no longer have H and R, merely R. Conditional therefore is a different logical operator in that is consider situations where the presence of (RH) and (H) leads to evidence for both H and R at the same time.

Calculating Conditional Probability

Finally we have the following probability formula directed by the elimination rule of conditionals. As usual we covert from predicates to sets.

(Prob(AB) Prob(B) = Prob(AB))

We have to be careful when using this formula because we tend to want to solve for (Prob(AB)) by dividing by (Prob(B)). However, doing so requires that we verify (Prob(B)). So be warned!

Example (Conditional)

What is the probability of drawing a 5 of Hearts and to have already drawn 1,2,3, & 4 of Hearts?

We have the events we need.

  • A is to draw 5 of Hearts.
  • B is to have drawn already 1,2,3,4 of hearts in some order. The probability of that event is (Prob(B)=1/270,725). (Trust me, but if you don’t then note that this is a subset of size 4 in a set of size 54 so there 54 choose 4 = 270,725 such subsets. We want only one of them.)

All things being fair, and treating the cards in other people’s hands as part of the deck of possible draws, then we have 52-4=48 cards left to draw of which one is the desired card, so 1/48 chance.

(Prob(AB) = 1/48).
