Quantifiers

\(\forall\), \(\exists\), \(=\), and \(!^n\)

Once more we can look to natural language to get a hint about what logical operators we will find useful. Think of giving out the same instruction to an ever growing list of employees:

Andrew, Brenda, Carlos, Diego, Ehud, Fari, and Georgio must wash their hands before preparing food.

There a number of disadvantages to this approach. For one thing we are letting natural language get away with some imprecise form. Notice there is only one use of the word AND yet the Language of AND works in pairs <this> AND <that>. So the proper form would need us to pair up the instructions for which we invent parentheses

( (Andrew AND (Brenda AND Carlos)) AND (Diego AND (Ehud AND Fari) ) AND Georgio must wash their hands before preparing food.

Of course this is one of many ways we could pair up the AND’s without actually making the logic any easier to explain.

Since logic is invented by humans for human use, it makes sense to create a version of AND which is much more forgiving about repeated use. This would allow us to get the idea into a precise language we could use in reasoning and making future programs. What should this “repeated AND” be called? Pause to think of how you might say the instructions in simpler way. Here are some options that come to my mind.

  • All employees must wash their hands before preparing food.
  • Each employee must wash their hands before preparing food.
  • Each and every employee must wash their hands before preparing food.

The natural languages we use on each other have many choices for style and emphasis. We can see the basic form is not working to avoid listing all the people. Instead it indexes them into a single category “Employee”. It states the rule they must obey “Wash hands” and then quantifies who in the employee list has this requirement: ALL/EACH/EVERY.

Tip

The word any is often tempting to use as a quantifier, but within natural language it can vasilate between mean all or some, which which quite a different meaning.

  • “All employees must wash their hands.”
  • “Did any employee wash their hands?”

As a habit you may do better to practice choosing a term that is less likely to be misunderstood.

The Universal Quantifier \(\forall\)

The logical operator “For all” is denoted \(\forall\) as an upside-down letter “A” (for “All”). To express it we follow the L.I.E.

Language of For All

The grammar of a universal quantifier is given by \[ \tag{$L_{\forall}$} \langle universal\rangle ::= \forall \langle \text{variable}\rangle . \langle\text{Predicate}\rangle \] In words we say “For all \(x\), \(P(x)\) is valid” where \(x\) is a variable and \(P(x)\) is a predicate. Variations include “\(P(x)\) holds, for all \(x\)” and “for every \(x\), \(P(x)\) is valid”.

Notice this is another example of binding a variable. So just as with substitution rules and the Paradox of the trapped variable, we need to be careful to rename \(x\) when substituting.

Proposition 1. \(\forall n.(n<n+1)\).

Here the variable is \(n\) and the predicate is \(n<n+1\).

It is not necessary that the predicate actually depend on the variable, for example

Proposition 2. \(\forall n. (2+2=4)\).

Elimination of For All

We have not forgotten how to spell LIE but we are skipping ahead to the elimination rule because this is what truly organizes what “for all” does. When the manager asserts that “All employees wash their hands!” the meaning is found by applying this claim to any single employee. For example this means “Brenda must wash her hands!” And a new employee “Henri must wash his hands!” Notice the application no longer features “All” nor a variable “employee”. Thus it is an elimination of the structure of the operator.

\[ \tag{$E_{\forall}$} \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma & \vdash \forall x.P(x)\\ \Gamma & \vdash c \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash P(c)\end{array} \]

In words:

If context \(\Gamma\) leads to “For all x, P(x) is valid” and context also leads to a value c for x, then P(c) must hold in \(\Gamma\).”

Introduction Rule for universal quantifiers

Now that we know how to use the “for all” operator we can guess what we need to know to discover an example of a “for all” operator. Basically we need to reverse what we want as a conclusion. We need to know that if context can make a constant value c for x lead to P(c) being valid, then context is saying P(x) is valid “for all x”. This is quite similar to turning a substitution into a function by function abstraction. The major difference is that instead of a function that output data we think of universal quantifiers and proving a proposition.

\[ \tag{$I_{\forall}$} \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma, c & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash \forall x.P(x)\end{array} \]

In software, a universal quantifier uses a computational judgement, hence evidence (data) must be produced for each \(P(x)\). In other words \(\forall x.P(x)\) in a Brouwerian judgment is synonymous with a function \(x\mapsto P\).

Notice the premise now has an unknown constant c which together with context leads to P(c). How do we come up with that? We do that through a mix of assumption (hypothesis, axioms, laws) and proofs. For example, a manager or a health inspector make a rule that all employees must wash their hands. Can they really know that this has always been obeyed? Perhaps not but in theory that is how it should work. Once we begin to work in practice or in computation we might find we need to relax our rules. We saw this already with AND where we derived new logic based on include partial truths. We can do the same with “for all” but in order that we call something a “for all” we should keep at least on feature that the theory exhibits. And that is the elimination rule.

For example, if we relax to accept that perhaps some employee neglects to wash their hands we should be within our rights to fire the employee and the health inspector can close the restaurant. When it comes to “for all” it is must important to protect the outcome that clean hands handle food than it is to protect the way introduce the law.

Existential Quantifier \(\exists\)

Language of existential quantifier

The grammar of an existential quantifier is given by \[ \tag{$L_{\exists}$} \langle existential\rangle ::= \exists \langle \text{variable}\rangle . \langle\text{Predicate}\rangle \] In words we say “There exists an \(x\) such that \(P(x)\) is valid” where \(x\) is a variable and \(P(x)\) is a predicate. Variations include “\(P(x)\) holds, for some \(x\)” and “there is at least one \(x\) such that \(P(x)\) is valid”.

Introduction rule for existential quantifiers

\[ \tag{$I_{\exists}$} \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash \exists x.P(x)\end{array} \]

Elimination rule for existential quantifiers

\[ \tag{$E_{\exists}$} \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma & \vdash \exists x.P(x)\\ \Gamma, c & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash Q\end{array} \] In words:
> If context \(\Gamma\) leads to “There exists an x such that P(x) is valid” and context also leads to a value c for x, then P(c) must hold in \(\Gamma\) and hence Q must hold in \(\Gamma\).

Equality

\(x=y\) means “\(x\) is identical to \(y\)” and can be expressed in terms of the universal quantifier as follows: \[ x=y \equiv \forall P.(P(x) \leftrightarrow P(y)) \] In other words, “\(x\) is identical to \(y\)” is equivalent to “for all predicates \(P\), \(P(x)\) is valid if, and only if, \(P(y)\) is valid”.

\(x=x\)

NoteProof

For a predicate \(P\) and data \(c\) in the context, if \(P(c)\) is valid then \(P(c)\) is valid, i.e.  \[ \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma, c & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma,c & \vdash P(c)\end{array} \]

$P(c)P(c). Hence by the introduction rule for the universal quantifier, \(\forall P.(P(x) \leftrightarrow P(x))\) is valid. By the Leibniz Law of Identity, \(x=x\) is valid.

Counting quantifiers and uniqueness

In affine logic we remove the option to duplicate assumptions as this can related to physical resources. In the stricter linear logic we further require the use of every assumption.

$! x.P(x) $ means “there exists exactly one x such that P(x) is valid”. This is a counting quantifier. It can be expressed in terms of the existential and universal quantifiers as follows:

In a classical logic, the uniqueness quantifier can be expressed as follows: \[ \exists ! x.P(x) \equiv \exists x.(P(x) \wedge \forall y.(P(y) \Rightarrow x=y)) \] In words, “there exists exactly one \(x\) such that \(P(x)\) is valid” is equivalent to “there exists an \(x\) such that \(P(x)\) is valid and for all \(y\), if \(P(y)\) is valid then \(y\) must be equal to \(x\)”.

Linear and Affine Logics

Meanwhile a rule like contraction clearly seems suspect in concepts like “have a USB port” or “have an egg” where counting seems crucial for the logic. A logic that cares about such counting is often called a “linear logic” (if it needs an exact match) or an “affine logic” (if it is upper bounded by a count). You can find in programming languages like Idris and Rust that allow for these logics. They are especially useful when we care to track that all our files are and memory are used and released as expected, and to boost the security guarantees of vulnerable software. It is hard to ease drop on data if you can only read it once.

The following Idris code shows how to specify that a value can only be used once. Hence the following fails because it used the value hasUSB twice because the preceeding number 1 instruct Idris to use that value exactly once.

canKeyAndMouse : (1 hasUSB : PortScanner) -> Bool
canKeyAndMouse hasUSB = hasUSB && hasUSB

It also fails if you do not use the value at all.

canKeyAndMouse : (1 hasUSB : PortScanner) 
                    -> Bool
canKeyAndMouse hasUSB = false

Idris is only happy if you use the inputs exactly once, so you need to give it to options. Each gets used exactly once and captures the idea of our logic.

canKeyAndMouse : (1 hasUSB : PortScanner,
                  1 hasOtherUSB : PortScanner)
                  -> Bool
canKeyAndMouse hasUSB hasOtherUSB = 
                    hasUSB && hasOtherUSB

Rust makes a similar idea practical with ownership. A value once used transfer ownership and in effect disappears and cannot be reused. This is why the following could would fail.

let data = String::from("Pa$$w0rd");
send_to_captain(data);
// Next line causes error; data moved.
send_to_criminal(data);

Here is a safe use of data.

let data = String::from("Pa$$w0rd");
send_to_captain(data);
// Next line causes error; data moved.
send_to_criminal(data);

Unlike Idris, Rust does not require you to use the value.

let data = String::from("Pa$$w0rd");  
// data never used, but that's ok

Though nothing should stop you from learning languages like Idris and Rust, this article should convince you firstly of valuable uses for different than expected logic. When your situation demands you can involve future study and coding assistants to select the programming language that supports your logic best.

Summary of quantifiers

Universal quantifier

In a context \(\Gamma\), given a predicate \(P(x)\), if every object \(c\) in the context \(\Gamma\) leads to \(P(c)\) being valid, \(\forall x.P(x)\) is valid. \[ \begin{aligned} \langle universal\rangle & ::= \forall \langle \text{variable}\rangle . \langle\text{Predicate}\rangle & (L_{\forall}) \\[5pt] & \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma, c & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash \forall x.P(x)\end{array} & (I_{\forall})\\[5pt] & \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma & \vdash \forall x.P(x)\\ \Gamma & \vdash c \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash P(c)\end{array} & (E_{\forall})\\ \end{aligned} \]

Existential quantifier.

In a context \(\Gamma\), given a predicate \(P(x)\), if there exists an object \(c\) in the context \(\Gamma\) that leads to \(P(c)\) being valid, \(\exists x.P(x)\) is valid. \[ \begin{aligned} \langle existential\rangle & ::= \exists \langle \text{variable}\rangle . \langle\text{Predicate}\rangle & (L_{\exists}) \\[5pt] & \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash \exists x.P(x)\end{array} & (I_{\exists})\\[5pt] & \begin{array}{rl} \Gamma & \vdash \exists x.P(x)\\ \Gamma, c & \vdash P(c) \\ \hline \Gamma & \vdash Q\end{array} & (E_{\exists})\\ \end{aligned} \]

Equality

\[ \begin{aligned} x=y & \equiv \forall P.(P(x) \leftrightarrow P(y)) & (\text{Leibniz Law of Identity})\\[5pt] \end{aligned} \]

Counting quantifiers

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle counting\rangle & ::= !^{\langle \mathbb{N}\rangle} \langle \text{variable}\rangle . \langle \text{Predicate}\rangle\\ \frac{\Gamma, c_1,\ldots, c_n\vdash P(c_1),\ldots,P(c_n)}{\Gamma\vdash !^{\leq n} x.P(x)}(I_{!^n})\\ \exists ! \langle \text{variable}\rangle . \langle\text{Predicate}\rangle \exists ! x.P(x) & \equiv \exists x.(P(x) \wedge \forall y.(P(y) \Rightarrow x=y)) & (\text{Counting quantifier})\\ \end{aligned} \]
